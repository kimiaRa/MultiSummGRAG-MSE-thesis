A Dream of Arianrhod Peace at last. My baby is sleeping, his quiet breathing barely audible, the soft light of our night lamp caressing his cheek with a warm glow. I sink back onto the bed. How many times has he woken up tonight? Six? Seven? How many nights of broken sleep have I gone through? Days. No, months. ‘I just want my body for me,’ I think, as I lie down, very carefully, so as not to disturb him. I close my eyes… Then jerk up again, as he begins, again, to cry. ‘I can’t go on like this,’ I think, as I take him in my arms and rock him until he is calm enough to take my nipple in his mouth. ‘I can’t go on,’ I think. I sigh. Through the window, I catch the silver gleam of the crescent moon, high in the deep night sky. I sigh again, and this time, whisper the words aloud. ‘I can’t go on like this.’ The silver light suddenly starts to glow brighter. And brighter. The whole room is now filled with searing white moonlight. I try to shield my baby’s eyes, but he has come off my breast and is now sleeping peacefully, completely unperturbed. I look around, shading my own eyes. The white light is moving now, coalescing until instead of filling the bedroom, it is concentrated in one spot on the wall just opposite me. Now the light is shining straight at me, an unbearable sight. I look down. ‘You can’t take it any more?’ says a voice. I look up. The light has taken shape, taken form. Standing before me is the figure of a beautiful woman. Her long red robes seem to flow about her legs, played with by unseen wind. Her golden hair appears equally wayward. Her eyes hold the darkness of the hollows of trees; of the cloud-filled Welsh night; of the inside of the womb. She stretches out her hand to me. Her fingers are decorated with many elaborate silver rings. I see that her head, too, is adorned, with a crescent of silver. ‘Who are you?’ I ask, whispering so that I don’t wake my son – but he continues to sleep peacefully. She smiles. Somehow, I do not like the smile. ‘If you can’t take him, I will,’ she says, and reaches out to the baby. I stare, too shocked to breathe for some moments. Then, ‘No!’ I cry, grabbing him and holding him so tightly that he must surely wake; yet he sleeps on. ‘Who do you think you are?’ I ask angrily, for she is still reaching out to my baby. I hold him tighter, murmuring soothing words. I look up and see that the beautiful woman has opened her beautiful mouth and is laughing, a beautiful, terrible sound. ‘Who am I?’ she asks, still laughing, ‘I think you need to ask, who are you? Are you a mother?’ I look down, sensing some trick. ‘I… of course,’ I say eventually. ‘So am I,’ she replies, ‘I am a mother. I am The Mother. I have many names. Arianrhod you may call me. Sons I had, but I wanted my body for me. I wanted an independent life. Come, give him to me. you will have your life back. You will be able to sleep. Perhaps I will even refrain from cursing him, as I did my own son…’ ‘Why would you curse your own son?’ I murmur, but she moves closer, still reaching out her hand, and I clutch my baby tighter. ‘Come now,’ she says, in tones which are suddenly so sweet that I find myself loosening my grip. But only slightly. ‘You will not be so different from all the mothers who turn away from their children, who follow my path,’ she says. ‘Those who need to separate themselves from their creations.’ ‘But I don’t…’ I begin, yet she interrupts. ‘Once upon a time,’ she says, ‘I lived with my children upon a hilltop, close to the singing of the deep blue sea. All my children were there, growing and loving and playing. Some children put roots down deep into the soil. Some burrowed beneath it. Some ran across the soft, lush grass, shouting and laughing in the sunshine. But I tired of being present for them. I turned away.’ She smiles, but there are tears in her eyes. ‘Now the hill is gone. Men came and cut it away, piece by piece. The grass is replaced with grey concrete, strangling the roots, and covering the skeletons of the burrowing creatures. Now no one runs and laughs there. The wind sighs through the hollows in the rocks, the sharp rocks next to the deep grey sea.’ She turns away, the crescent on her head flashing. I sit still, pondering the meaning of her words. Turning back, she reaches her hand out once more. ‘Give him to me,’ she says. ‘All those whose mothers turn away from them belong to me now. They will have no names, they will not be able to fight or love; their laughter will stop at the level of their eyes. But their mothers are free. Come, there is nothing you can do. Gwydion tries with his Earth-magic to trick me, to bring back the names, but I will not give them. I will give nothing now.’ At the words ‘earth magic’ I feel something begin to change within me. The fear and anger start to dissolve. I feel the light of the moon shining upon my milk-filled breast. I feel my feet connecting to the bed, and that connection reaching down, through the earth, joining with the roots of trees, joining with all the world. I feel my power. ‘No,’ I say. She stretches upwards, growing taller and taller until her head reaches the ceiling. Glaring down at me with her flashing dark eyes, she says, ‘It’s your choice. I ask you once more. Give the child to me, and enjoy your freedom.’ My body is pulsing with energy; the energy of the stars, of budding flowers, ready to open; the energy of creation itself. ‘No,’ I say again. She laughs again, yet this time the laugh seems more beautiful, less terrible. There is a bright glow all around me. And then she is gone. I look down at my baby, who slept soundly through the whole thing. I am still exhausted. But I see that, to him, I am immensely powerful. And that power fills me with an exhilarating joy. So what if I have a few sleepless nights? I am doing the most sacred and loving task a woman can do. So what if I am low in energy? I have the waxing moon to help me; the rising tree-sap; the waves of the ceaseless ocean. I am filled with sacred, creative power. I am ready to dance, to sing, to make wild love. I am free. And while I am deciding which one to do first, I sink back onto the pillows and fall asleep. Because, wild creative energy or not, sometimes it does feel really good to rest. Call me Lu: A Lammas Tale We are the ones who live under the earth. We are the whispers in the trees and the feel of soft rain upon your face. We live in the lightning arcing across the sky, in deep pools in the mountains, on the other side of the rainbow. We are the sparkles at the edge of your vision, the haze in the air, the place where you thought you could see the edge of the horizon, but it actually continues beyond your normal sight. We were once less shy of humans, long ago, before we split into different tribes, before the humans became so careless that we retreated, into the hills and behind men’s smiles. There are many tales of the times when we were great, of the times when we were the Great Ones, and all the green earth was ours to play in. At the midpoint between summer and autumn there came a very great king of the great ones. He, like us, has many names. I remember long before he became the king, before he had even visited the magical realm. I still remember as though it was yesterday. It was, in fact, tomorrow. In the hill which concealed our Hall, I kept the gate. I am the watcher. And watching that day, that fine summer day on the midpoint between summer and autumn, I saw him. I saw him coming to the foot of the hill. This was long before he became king of the Tribe of Gods, long before anyone even saw the divine light within him. He seemed like just another human, blundering along as they do, looking all around but not seeing anything, not the carven gate or the shining hall within; seeing only the green grass of the hill. He started walking up the hill. The summer sun was shining on his long, fair hair. He came walking, as they do, up the slope. Then he stopped right before the gate, and that is when I saw his eyes. Clear, deep eyes, and he turned his head from side to side, and then looked straight at me. ‘Well,’ I said, ‘there’s not many human folk who can walk right up to the gate! What is it you want?’ He looked at me some moments. Then, ‘let me in,’ he said. No polite greetings, no questions, and not even any gifts he offered. In those days, of course, humans gave us gifts. Nowadays we still make the light of summer make sparkles around their food, and whisper to them through the leaves of the trees. But mostly they do not see, they do not hear. They forget us, and care not for our homes. ‘I see, I hear,’ said this one then, ‘I do not forget. Let me in, for my mother was one of you. Let me in to the revels.’ ‘You may pass,’ I told him, ‘if you tell me your name.’ He hesitated then. I could see he was not sure. I smiled. Human folk always want to seem like they are right, even when they so rarely are. ‘Call me Lu,’ he said. I looked to the gate. It continued to be closed and locked. I looked back to the man. ‘Not enough,’ I smiled. He looked disconcerted, but only for a moment. Then, ‘Call me Lugh,’ he said again, sounding as though he was merely repeating what he had said before. But I heard the silent ‘g’, calling the fields and the sweet grass of late summer; the ‘h’ of transformation and generation. The gate opened. ‘What will you do in our merry halls?’ I asked. ‘I can play the sweetest music you have ever heard,’ he responded, as he passed through into the Hall. ‘I can fix anything that is broken, I can throw a spear and always hit my mark, I can sing so as to bring tears even to your eyes.’ ‘Pah! Enough boasting,’ I said, waving him through, ‘you will find plenty of folk here who will show you not to speak so freely.’ And though it is true that, on that occasion, many of the folk who challenged him did win, this only made him more determined. And by the time he became king, he had all kinds of other names and many a tale was told of him. They say his mother, angry that he left the human’s realm, set a curse upon him to never be able to marry a woman born on earth, so his uncle made him a wife of sweet flowers. They say he led a battle against an army of demons and defeated them with a single stroke of his spear. They say, they say… But of course, that could have been a different Lu. We are still here, reminding you to give thanks for the bread you make from your harvests, reminding you that there is always more to life than meets the eye. Perhaps one day you will slow down and start to see. Perhaps even one of you will come walking boldly into our world. But then you will need the power of Lugh to help you. Is up down? Everything is glowing with a soft purplish light which fades into dark blue at the corner of my eyes. I don’t know why I am here. All I know is that I have to stop the balls from floating away. They are all around me, soft, black globes glittering faintly from within. They fly around my head and slowly start drifting away. It is vitally important that they do not drift away. Without stoping to think why, I jump and make a grab for the little globes. If I catch them in just the right way – not too hard, not too soft- I can hold them. Too hard, and they bounce away from me. Too soft, and my fingers rip right through them like smoke. Hurriedly, I begin gathering as many as I can to my chest. I am not even aware that I am more or less swimming through the strange, glowing air. My only thought is to catch as many as I can. ‘Wouldn’t you prefer one like this?’ says a voice close to me. I flap myself around, still clinging desperately to the balls. There is a figure there who I know. Despite the apparent lack of any surfaces, he is sitting, cross-legged. I see the deep blue-black of his throat and the wild waving of his hair. As I look at him, he smiles, and pulls one of my balls from the air. Immediately, the ball changes. Black smoke peels off from all around it, revealing a perfectly round crystalline sphere. I look at it. It is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen. On the outside it is clear, but within its depths I see all the colours of the world, swirling, sparkling, shining. I look up into the god’s eyes. It is like looking into an abyss. I catch my breath and look down at the crystal again. He is smiling. ‘I… Can’t I have all of them at the same time?’ I say. He shrugs his shoulders. Arthur and the Wild Forest Lady A Summer Solstice Tale Scarlet blood dripping from her fangs, breaking branches as she stalks through the trees, matted hair flying out behind her, eyes like shadowy pools staring through the leaves, she comes. She comes snarling and cursing and growling. She veers this way and that way, small animals scuttling out of her path. She passes between the trees like a tempestuous wind, into the glade. There stand Arthur and Gawain. Arthur is sad because, last Midsummer, he shot the white deer who was the forest’s guardian. The Forest Spirit was angry, and ordered Arthur to give his life in exchange for that of the deer. Knowing that Arthur usually respects and honours the forest, the Forest Spirit offered Arthur a way out. ‘I will take your life in exchange for the deer in one year and one day,’ the Forest Spirit had said, ‘Unless you can answer me this riddle: what do women want?’ Now the days are lengthening once again, and all his months of searching have not availed Arthur of the answer to this most puzzling riddle. When he first returned home to his court, the first person he asked for help was his wife, Guinevere. ‘What do women want?’ she had said, ‘I’ll tell you what I want. I want a new hat, and I want a beach holiday, and I want my love of polyamory to be respected, and I want people to listen to me…’ The list went on and on. Arthur knew better than to interrupt. But he also knew that none of these things was the answer to the riddle. Even with the help of his friend Gawain, who has been travelling with him these past months and who certainly has a lot more experience with the ladies, he still has no idea of the answer. The forest glade they stand in is green and pleasant. The ripening summer sun shines down on flowers of many different colours, and the air is filled with the soft humming of bees. But Arthur is looking down at the ground, almost in despair. Gawain puts his hand on his friend’s shoulder and begins murmuring words of encouragement, when he stops, and both look up, as they hear the sounds. Leaves rustling. branches breaking. Creatures scampering. And through the trees, she comes. She comes. Arthur leaps up, hand on his sword-hilt. He is about to draw the sword, but he recalls that it was just this quickness to use a weapon which had got him into this mess in the first place. When he saw that beautiful white deer prancing through the forest, he had fitted arrow to bow and loosed it all in one swift motion without thinking. It was only afterwards, as he came to the magnificent creature, shot right through the heart, that he began to have doubts. And then the Forest Spirit had appeared… So, though he is filled with dread, this time he stays his hand. Both men watch as she approaches them. A strong odour of wolf-fur and pine-needles washes through the glade. Arthur gathers his courage and speaks, trying not to let his voice shake. ‘Who are you?’ She who has many names; Kali, Ceridwen, Hecate, she answers in this tale with a voice like the deep rumbling of the rocks under earth, ‘I am Ragnall’. Arthur is about to ask more, but then Ragnall speaks again. ‘I know who you are, and I know what you seek. You seek the answer to the riddle.’ ‘Oh, yes, I do!’ says Arthur, ‘but I’ – ‘I can tell you the answer’, says Ragnall. ‘You can? Oh, that would be’ – ‘I will tell you. If…’ ‘If…?’ repeats Arthur, a sinking feeling in his soul. ‘If,’ says Ragnall, ‘You marry me.’ Now as we know, Arthur already has a wife, and much as she would like to have another husband, the rules of the court at that time bade that no one be married to more than one at a time. So, in his best courtly tone, he says, ‘Oh great lady, though I am truly honoured by such a proposition, lamentably I am already bound to a wife and as such cannot undertake such a wonderful and delightful offer.’ Ragnall is still. Her eyes flash. ‘However,’ Arthur continues, ‘my friend here is still single…’ ‘What?’ asks Gawain. ‘Please, do this for me,’ Arthur asks him in an undertone. And some say that it was indeed for the love of his friend, who Gawain wished to save, that Gawain stepped forward. But some say that as he looked at Ragnall, at her face like that of a beast, her arms like tree roots, her breast heaving with savage energy, some say that he saw a kind of beauty there. ‘Yes,’ says Gawain. They have to be quick, for the year and a day is nearly over, and Arthur needs to find out the answer to the riddle before the Forest Spirit takes his life. So they return home and arrange the wedding for the coming days. There is a magnificent party, with feasting, and dancing, and much merriment. And if anyone is surprised by Gawain’s choice of wife, they are of course far too polite to mention it. On the wedding night, Gawain and Ragnall leave the partygoers to their revelries, and go up to their bedroom. There, Ragnall asks Gawain to kiss her. He knew that this would be coming, but it is still a daunting thing. But he takes a deep breath, brings his lips to her hard, stinking mouth, and kisses her. Suddenly the lips touching his become as soft as rose petals. He steps back in surprise and sees that Ragnall is changed. Her skin is soft and glowing, her hair falls sleekly and elegantly around her shoulders, her eyes are shining with a gentle light. She is the most beautiful woman Gawain has ever seen. ‘The spell is broken,’ says Ragnall, and as she speaks, a wondrous perfume floats around the room. ‘You mean, now you will be beautiful always?’ asks Gawain, hardly daring to believe his luck. ‘Ah,’ she says, ‘Not quite.’ ‘I knew it,’ he mutters. ‘Now I can be beautiful and soft and gentle and kind in the daytime, and wild and crazy and fierce at night,’ says Ragnall, ‘ Or I can be wild and crazy and fierce in the daytime, and beautiful and soft and gentle and kind at night. Fair by day and foul by night… Or foul by day and fair by night. Which do you choose?’ Gawain stands thinking. If Ragnall was beautiful and soft and gentle and kind in the daytime, he could hang out with his friends with her. She would be able to make him meals, and keep their castle clean. But then at the end of the day, just when he wanted to relax, there she would be, the wild, fierce hag. Or she could be like that in the daytime, but then he would never be able to take her anywhere. And she certainly wouldn’t do any cleaning. But then at night, he would have her to himself, in her soft, gentle form… Round and round he goes, but he cannot decide which would be better. Then eventually he realises something. ‘This is my wife,’ he thinks, ‘But it’s her life’. And turning to Ragnall he says, ‘Lady, the choice must be yours.’ ‘You’ve solved the riddle!’ she cries. Gawain stands astounded. ‘The answer to the question of what women want?’ he asks. ‘What women want, and indeed what everyone wants,’ she replies, ‘Is simply to choose for themselves. By recognising this you have given me back my power of choice. Now, when I want to appear beautiful and soft and gentle and kind, that I will be, and when I want to be wild and crazy and fierce, that I will be.’ Gawain, overcome with happiness, is eager to get to know his new beautiful wife, but he knows that first he has to tell Arthur the answer. And it seems he got there in time, for the Forest Spirit spared Arthur’s life. And, as the tales go, they all lived happily ever after. * My thanks to Elen Sentier for her version of this tale. Persephone looked at the cherry-bowl longingly, and Hades took the opportunity while her attention was not on him to turn his face away, so she did not see the fruit wither and rot in his mouth. “I’m not sure…” said Persephone. She remembered, being a sensible young Goddess, all the stories her mother had told her about travellers who end up in the land of the fairies, and who, once they eat the food of that land, are doomed to stay there forever. But this did not seem like a fairyland… “How about a pomegranate?” asked Hades, holding out the bowl to her. Now, pomegranates were Persephone’s absolute favourite fruit. As she looked at the pomegranates in the silver bowl, she felt so hungry that she could not take her eyes from them. She did not even register the sound of distant crashing from far above. “Well…” she said, slowly reaching out her hand – but then hesitated. “You don’t need to eat the whole fruit, how about just a few seeds?” suggested Hades; “look how juicy they are. I only got the best for you.” “Well…OK…” she replied, taking some seeds in her hand. She brought them to her mouth. She put them to her lips. She chewed, she swallowed. At that moment Demeter came thundering into the garden, and pointed a terrible finger at Hades. “Give me back my daughter!” she cried. “Hey, she came here of her own free will!” protested Hades. Persephone, looking up, frowned at her mother. Why was she so angry? “I’m not a child, you know, mother,” she pointed out. Demeter looked from Hades to Persephone, trying to figure out what was going on. Persephone seemed safe and under no great threat – but there was something suspiciously confident about Hades that she did not like at all. “I know, my daughter,” she said, “but wouldn’t like to come home now to the land of the trees and the sky?” “Yes!” said Persephone, leaping up from the table and rushing to her mother. She tried to take Demeter’s hand, but something stopped her – there seemed to be some kind of invisible barrier separating them. “What’s happening?” she asked, afraid again. Hades glided up to her, attempting a sweet and soothing smile which looked to Persephone quite sickly. “It’s just that you already ate the fruit,” he pointed out. “You what?!” cried Demeter. Hades threw up his hands as if it had nothing at all to do with him. “Hey, you know the rules,” he said. “I didn’t make them, but there they are. She ate the fruit, so now she has to stay here…with me,” he added, grinning more wolfishly than ever at Persephone. But she turned away, tears splashing from her eyes. “Forever?” she asked, not looking at Hades but at Demeter; “forever, mother?” Demeter was also crying and glaring at Hades. As the Goddesses’ tears fell onto the black ground, there sprouted tiny flowers, which then faded and died. “But it was just a few pomegranate seeds!” wailed Persephone. “A few?” asked Demeter, seizing on this piece of information. “Oh no,” Hades interjected, “just because she only ate six seeds, doesn’t make any difference – you know the rules…” He trailed off, realising he had, in fact, lost. Demeter waved her hand through the air in a complicated sigil. There was a bright flash, and then she took Persephone’s hand. “Come, daughter,” she said. “But – hey, wait!” objected Hades, “I mean, she ate the fruit!” “She did eat the fruit,” agreed Demeter, “so she shall come here for the dark part of every year; one month for each seed. And you,” she added, glaring down at Hades, “have six months to convince her that she wants to join you down here.” “I’m sure it won’t be that bad,” said Persephone, who had brightened up knowing she could return home now, and was secretly trying to think of a way out once they had escaped the Underworld. And still holding her mother’s hand, she went back up to world, where spring was just beginning. Hades sulked for some time after this, but eventually realised that the compromise was a pretty good one, for him. Persephone didn’t tell me how she changed her mind but when autumn came, she went happily down with her new King to his domain. And she continues to do so still.
